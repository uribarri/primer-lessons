The Library of Rama Lama

Being a Hearthside Gathering & Recitation of Mr. Edward Lear's "The Owl and the Pussy-Cat"

Chapter I — The Hearthside Welcome

Outside the tall arched windows of the grand sanctuary, a soft autumn drizzle tapped gently against the leaded glass. Inside, however, the atmosphere was bathed in golden warmth. High wooden shelves of carved mahogany stretched all the way to the vaulted ceiling, filled to overflowing with ancient volumes, stitched maps, and gild-edged manuscripts. The air smelled delightfully of aged parchment, beeswax candles, and sweet mountain cedar.

Near the crackling fireplace sat Rama Lama in his cushioned armchair. His dark brown curly wool looked soft and inviting in the firelight, his distinctive curved cream horns swept back with noble quietude, and the single turquoise bead of his cord necklace glimmered like a calm star upon his chest. Hearing a footstep at the heavy oak doorway, he looked up, his dark eyes brimming with gentle warmth.

"Ah, welcome, my young friend!" Rama Lama said softly, rising to offer a courteous bow. "Please, step inside out of the damp air and draw your chair close to the hearth. You have arrived at a most opportune moment. I was just retrieving one of my very favorite volumes from the poetry alcove."

He held up a handsome, gold-embossed book bound in emerald cloth. "Poetry," Rama Lama observed thoughtfully as he settled back into his armchair, "is a quiet kind of magic. While prose explains the world to our minds, a poem speaks directly to the imagination. It brings us rhythm, music, and gentle wonder. Today, I should like to read to you a famous poem that never fails to bring joy to my heart: The Owl and the Pussy-Cat, written by Mr. Edward Lear."

Chapter II — The Recitation of the Verse

Rama Lama smoothed the pages of the emerald volume with great care. He adjusted his posture, took a quiet breath, and began to read aloud in his deep, soothing, and melodious voice:

The Owl and the Pussy-Cat

By Edward Lear

I

The Owl and the Pussy-cat went to sea

In a beautiful pea-green boat;

They took some honey, and plenty of money

Wrapped up in a five-pound note.

The Owl looked up to the moon above,

And sang to a small guitar,

"O lovely Pussy! O Pussy, my love!

What a beautiful Pussy you are,—

You are,

What a beautiful Pussy you are!"

II

Pussy said to the Owl, "You elegant fowl!

How wonderful sweet you sing!

Oh, let us be married,—too long we have tarried,—

But what shall we do for a ring?"

They sailed away for a year and a day

To the land where the Bong-tree grows,

And there in a wood a piggy-wig stood

With a ring in the end of his nose,—

His nose,

With a ring in the end of his nose.

III

"Dear Pig, are you willing to sell for one shilling

Your ring?" Said the piggy, "I will."

So they took it away, and were married next day

By the turkey who lives on the hill.

They dined upon mince and slices of quince,

Which they ate with a runcible spoon,

And hand in hand on the edge of the sand

They danced by the light of the moon,—

The moon,

They danced by the light of the moon.

Chapter III — Rama Lama's Reflections

As the final words echoed softly in the quiet room, Rama Lama gently rested the open book upon his lap. He looked into the glowing embers of the fireplace, a warm and peaceful smile resting upon his face.

"Ah," Rama Lama murmured softly, turnings his dark, expressive eyes back to the reader. "Every time I hear those lines, I feel as though a refreshing ocean breeze has swept into the library. Do you know why I enjoy this poem so very much? Allow me to share three reasons."

1. The Harmony of Unlikely Friends

"Consider who has set sail together! An Owl, belonging to the night sky, and a Cat, belonging to the green earth. In the ordinary world, one might expect them to remain distant strangers. Yet here, they share their honey, sing to one another under the stars, and embark on a grand journey in complete trust. It reminds me that true affection and friendship do not depend on coming from the same place or being alike. When hearts are gentle, even the most different creatures can build a home of joy together."

2. The Playful Music of Language

"Notice how Mr. Lear plays with words! Words like runcible spoon or the Bong-tree are not found in heavy dictionaries, yet they feel completely right in our imagination. The poem bounces with rhythm, like a merry tune played on a small guitar. It teaches us that language is not merely for exchanging facts; it can be used to play, to sing, and to bring lighthearted delight to our spirit."

3. The Courage to Voyage Together

"Lastly, I admire their cheerful courage. They did not hesitate to sail away for a year and a day into the unknown. They brought what little they had—a bit of honey, a five-pound note, a guitar, and their devotion to one another—and they found happiness waiting for them on the distant sand. It reassures us that when we face new horizons with goodwill, kindness, and music, we will always find our way to dance by the light of the moon."

"Thank you for sharing this quiet hour with me," Rama Lama said gently, closing the emerald volume with a satisfying click. "Whenever the world outside feels a little too hurried, remember that you may always step into your imagination—or return here to my library—and set sail in a pea-green boat of your own."
